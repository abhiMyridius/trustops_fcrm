Cover · about 0:15

  • One sentence on what it is, one on the stance: AI proposes, people decide, a deterministic engine calculates the score. Then move on.
  • The repository is the primary submission; these slides are the guided tour of how the AI was built and governed.
  • The diagram on the right is the whole idea in one picture: AI proposes, a person decides, code scores. The cards at the bottom jump straight to each part. Hand over to the BA for the problem.

Slide 2 · The problem · BA · about 1:00

  • We are considering a US national bank and 6 change types: new product, new feature, process change, vendor or third party, new geography and new customer segment.
  • We mapped these to the FFIEC exam manual's risk categories, which examiners across the US use to assess AML, CFT and sanctions risk. (Point at the left diagram: each change lands in one or two of the four domains.)
  • Today the process is manual and inconsistent. The product owner gathers information from their own experience, so it is often incomplete, which means back and forth with the analyst. The analyst assesses from their own experience and judgement, so different analysts reach different assessments. And there is limited traceability to why a recommendation was made.
  • So what we set out to do is one traceable assessment from intake to a final recommendation, with AI as an assistant, or leading the step where that earns its place, and a person deciding every item. (Walk the bottom strip left to right.)
"Why FFIEC?" It is the published supervisory framework US examiners actually use, so the decomposition is grounded, not invented. "Where does AI lead?" Reading documents and finding policy: judgement over text, always with a source, always confirmed by a person. Scores never come from AI.

Slide 4 · The AI that built it · about 1:20

  • We built it the way we'd want an AI team to work: one lead agent plans, writes briefs and merges; subagents do the work in their own worktrees; a person approves the plan and every decision.
  • Walk the diagram left to right: operator, lead, lanes, main. 109 agents in 56 hours, up to 11 at once.
  • Memory is files, not chat. Every agent starts fresh and reads the same CLAUDE.md rules, the brief and the decision log, so context doesn't drift. Docs were extracted once into the scratchpad.
  • Modular: we cloned 8 components from our AEO platform instead of rebuilding them.
  • 6.4 billion tokens sounds big, but 98% were cache re-reads at a tenth of the price. The model only wrote 3.8 million tokens.
  • Gates at every stage, and hooks make the commit gate automatic. 112 problems caught, 7 before any code.
"How did build agents fail?" 5 logged incidents, e.g. worktrees committed as embedded repos, and a fix that skipped app startup silently. Each one became a rule in the brief or a hook.

Slide 3 · The AI inside it · about 1:30

  • Follow the green dot: people at the top, AI in the middle, code at the bottom. Every AI step ends at a person, and every number comes from the code lane.
  • Inside every call: 3 guards before the model, one call, 2 after, then a critic on another model family and a person. On failure: one retry, then a person takes over.
  • Model choice: the cheapest model that passes its eval gate. Haiku reads, Sonnet judges and writes, Nova Pro critiques Claude's work.
  • Memory: four explicit kinds, all with sources. No hidden agent memory.
  • Zero trust: deny by default, read-only tools, and machines can't decide.
"Why no agent framework?" Pydantic AI runs one call; our own state machine owns the flow, so the whole flow reads in one file and every step is deterministic.

Slide 5 · Proof · about 1:00

  • Our demo case had 2 documents: that is the small case, measured live at 115k tokens, about 31 cents.
  • What grows with size is reading: one cheap Haiku job per document, about 2 cents each. The expensive Sonnet judgement reads capped packs and stops at 4, so a 100-document case is about $3, not $15.
  • The formula is on the slide: tokens times price per model. The 250k cap is one setting; today it fits about 11 documents, and bigger cases get their own cap.
  • Testing runs from 7,707 unit tests up to live AI evals and 12 kill-and-restart drills, plus deliberate attacks: injection, PII, hostile input, outages.
  • The drills: we killed the workers, the broker, the database and the API mid-job on the live stack. 12 of 12 pass, nothing lost or repeated, and they found 2 real bugs.
"What bugs did the drills find?" An access policy that was never reloaded after a restart, and a retry window shorter than an API restart. Both fixed. "Is 250k enough for big cases?" Not for 10+ documents. The cap is a per-case setting; a case that would pass it waits for a person and is never cut, so raising it is a config change with a decision row.
