## BSA/AML Risk Assessment

### FFIEC-BRA-01 Structure of an Enterprise Risk Assessment

Banks should maintain a documented financial-crime risk assessment that identifies the main categories of inherent risk across the institution, including customers, products and services, geographies and delivery channels. The assessment should describe the methodology used, the data sources relied on, and the reasoning behind each rating. Senior management should review the results, and the board should receive a summary so that resources and controls can be aligned with the risks the bank actually faces.

### FFIEC-BRA-02 Assessing Customer Risk Factors

Banks should evaluate inherent risk arising from the customer base by grouping customers according to relevant characteristics rather than treating all relationships alike. Useful factors include ownership complexity, business type, expected transaction volumes, use of intermediaries, and the presence of politically exposed individuals. The bank should quantify how many relationships fall into higher-risk groups, explain why those groups were chosen, and revisit the groupings when the customer mix shifts materially.

### FFIEC-BRA-03 Product and Service Inherent Risk Review

Banks should assess each product and service for features that could allow illicit funds to move quickly, anonymously or across borders. Relevant considerations include transfer speed, transaction limits, the ability to fund or withdraw through third parties, and the degree of customer anonymity. New products and significant feature changes should receive a documented risk review before launch, with expected controls identified and accepted by accountable management.

### FFIEC-BRA-04 Geographic Exposure Analysis

Banks should analyse the geographic footprint of their customers, counterparties and transaction flows to understand where exposure to money laundering and sanctions risk is concentrated. The analysis should consider domestic areas designated as higher risk as well as foreign jurisdictions with weak controls, significant corruption, or elevated illicit activity. Banks should document the sources used to rate locations and update ratings when conditions or business activity change.

### FFIEC-BRA-05 Delivery Channel and Third-Party Risk

Banks should consider how customers are onboarded and served when rating inherent risk. Non-face-to-face relationships, remote account opening, reliance on introducers, and outsourced service providers can weaken visibility into who is using the bank. Management should assess whether identity verification and monitoring remain effective through each channel, and should evaluate vendors supporting these activities before onboarding them and periodically thereafter.

### FFIEC-BRA-06 Residual Risk and Assessment Updates

Banks should compare inherent risk ratings with the strength of their controls to reach a residual risk conclusion for each risk area. Where residual risk exceeds the bank's stated appetite, management should set remediation actions with owners and deadlines. The assessment should be refreshed on a regular cycle and also when events occur that change the risk profile, such as entering a new market, launching a product, or changing a key process.

### FFIEC-BRA-07 Data Inputs and Quality for Risk Assessment

Banks should ensure that the data used to build a risk assessment is complete, accurate and traceable to its source systems. Examiners would expect management to identify the data elements behind each inherent-risk measure, document known gaps or manual adjustments, and test extracts for consistency before results are relied upon. Where data is unavailable for a business line, the bank should state the limitation openly and apply conservative assumptions rather than treating missing information as low risk.

### FFIEC-BRA-08 Board and Senior Management Oversight of Results

Banks should present risk assessment results to the board and senior management in a form that supports informed decisions. Reporting should summarize the main inherent risks, the strength of related controls, the resulting residual ratings and any actions that remain open. Senior management should be accountable for approving the assessment, challenging unexpected conclusions, and tracking remediation to completion, while the board should receive enough detail to judge whether the bank operates within its stated risk appetite.

### FFIEC-BRA-09 Independent Validation of Scoring Methodology

Banks should subject the methodology used to score inherent risk and control effectiveness to periodic independent review. The reviewer should be free of day-to-day responsibility for the assessment and should evaluate whether factor weightings are reasoned, whether rating thresholds separate materially different risks, and whether results behave sensibly when inputs change. Findings should be reported to management, and the bank should record how each weakness in the methodology was addressed or why it was accepted.

### FFIEC-BRA-10 Linking Risk Assessment to Monitoring Calibration

Banks should use the conclusions of the risk assessment to shape how transaction monitoring and related detection activity are designed and tuned. Areas rated higher in risk should receive more granular scenarios, lower tolerance for unexplained activity and closer review of alerts, while lower-risk areas may justify simpler coverage. Management should be able to show how specific assessment findings led to specific monitoring changes and should reassess coverage when the assessment identifies a new typology or exposure.

### FFIEC-BRA-11 Documenting Rationale for Risk Ratings

Banks should keep clear written support for every rating assigned within the risk assessment. Documentation should explain which facts and data were considered, how judgment was applied where evidence was mixed, and who approved the final conclusion. A knowledgeable reader who was not involved in the exercise should be able to follow the reasoning from inherent exposure through control assessment to the residual result, and understand why the bank reached that rating.

### FFIEC-BRA-12 Resource Allocation Aligned to Risk Priorities

Banks should align compliance staffing, technology investment and training effort with the priorities that emerge from the risk assessment. When the assessment shows rising exposure in a business line, management should consider whether investigators, analysts and systems are sufficient to manage it, and should document decisions to add or redeploy capacity. Shortfalls that cannot be closed quickly should be escalated, with interim compensating measures recorded and reviewed until resources are adequate.

### FFIEC-BRA-13 Risk Assessment Triggers for Business Change

Banks should reassess their money laundering and terrorist financing risk profile whenever a significant business change occurs, rather than waiting for the next scheduled review. Launching a new product or feature, altering a core process, entering a new market, or adding a new customer segment can shift inherent risk and weaken existing controls. Management should evaluate the change before launch, record the expected effect on risk ratings, and confirm that monitoring, staffing, and training are adequate for the altered profile.

### FFIEC-BRA-14 Aggregating Risk Across Business Lines

Banks should consolidate risk assessment results from individual business lines, affiliates, and branches into a coherent enterprise view so that concentrations and gaps are visible. Examiners expect management to look beyond isolated unit ratings and consider how the same customer, product, or counterparty may present exposure in several areas at once. The consolidation method should be consistent, reasonably transparent, and able to show why a combined rating differs from the ratings of its components.

## Customer Due Diligence

### FFIEC-CDD-01 Risk-Based Customer Profile at Onboarding

Banks should develop a customer profile at account opening that captures the nature and purpose of the relationship, expected activity, products requested, and the customer's occupation or business type. The profile should be sufficiently detailed to allow the bank to understand what normal activity looks like and to recognize departures from it. Information collected should be proportionate to the risk the customer presents and should be documented in a form that monitoring staff can readily use.

### FFIEC-CDD-02 Beneficial Ownership Identification for Legal Entities

Banks should identify and take reasonable steps to verify the natural persons who own or control legal entity customers. Management should understand ownership layers, recognize structures that obscure control, and escalate cases where owners cannot be identified or where the stated structure lacks a plausible business rationale. Procedures should explain how ownership information is refreshed and how discrepancies found later in the relationship are resolved and recorded.

### FFIEC-CDD-03 Enhanced Due Diligence for Higher-Risk Customers

Banks should apply enhanced due diligence to customers whose risk rating indicates greater exposure to money laundering or terrorist financing. Additional measures may include corroborating source of funds and source of wealth, understanding the expected counterparties, obtaining senior management approval to proceed, and performing more frequent reviews. The depth of inquiry should be commensurate with the risk and the rationale for conclusions should be retained in the customer file.

### FFIEC-CDD-04 Ongoing Monitoring and Periodic Profile Refresh

Banks should conduct ongoing monitoring to maintain and update customer information and to identify and report suspicious transactions. Refresh cycles should follow customer risk, with higher-risk relationships reviewed more often, and should also be triggered by events such as unusual activity, adverse information, or a material change in ownership or business model. Monitoring results should feed back into the customer risk rating in a documented way.

### FFIEC-CDD-05 Geographic Risk in Customer Risk Rating

Banks should consider geographic exposure when rating customers, including residence, place of incorporation, operating locations, and the destinations or origins of expected funds flows. Jurisdictions with weak anti-money laundering frameworks, heightened corruption, sanctions exposure, or significant illicit finance activity warrant greater scrutiny. Management should define how geographic factors are weighted, review the country list regularly, and ensure new corridors are assessed before activity begins.

### FFIEC-CDD-06 Reliance on Third Parties for Due Diligence

Banks that use vendors or other third parties to collect or verify customer information should remain accountable for the quality of the resulting due diligence. Management should assess the provider's capabilities before engagement, define required data standards and escalation rules in writing, test outputs on a sample basis, and monitor service performance. Deficiencies should be remediated promptly, and the bank should be able to retrieve underlying records on request.

### FFIEC-CDD-07 Politically Exposed Person Identification and Review

Banks should establish procedures to identify customers, beneficial owners and controlling persons who hold or have recently held prominent public functions, as well as their close family members and associates. Identification should occur at onboarding and again during periodic screening. Where a match is confirmed, banks should obtain information on source of wealth and source of funds, require senior management approval to establish or continue the relationship, and apply heightened monitoring proportionate to the exposure to corruption and misuse of public funds.

### FFIEC-CDD-08 Governance and Validation of Customer Risk Rating Models

Banks should govern their customer risk rating methodology as a managed model. Management should document the factors used, the weight given to each, and the rationale for score thresholds. The methodology should be independently validated on a regular cycle and after material changes to products or customer populations. Validation should test whether ratings separate higher-risk from lower-risk customers in practice, and overrides of model output should be limited, justified in writing and reviewed by a second person.

### FFIEC-CDD-09 Non-Face-to-Face Onboarding and Digital Identity Verification

Banks that open relationships remotely should recognise that the absence of in-person contact can raise inherent risk. Controls should include reliable document authentication, matching of the applicant to the presented identity, device and location consistency checks, and detection of synthetic or stolen identities. Banks should set out when a remote applicant must be routed to additional verification, and should review the performance of digital onboarding tools, including false acceptance rates, at regular intervals.

### FFIEC-CDD-10 Event-Driven Reviews of Customer Due Diligence

In addition to scheduled refreshes, banks should define events that prompt an unscheduled review of a customer's due diligence file. Examples include a sudden change in transaction patterns, a change of ownership or control, adverse media, a law enforcement inquiry, or the filing of a suspicious activity report. The bank should set timeframes for completing these reviews, record the outcome, and update the risk rating and the expected activity profile where the findings warrant it.

### FFIEC-CDD-11 Adverse Media and Public Information Screening

Banks should use reputable public information sources to identify negative news linked to customers, beneficial owners and key counterparties. Screening should be proportionate to risk, cover financial crime allegations as well as sanctions and regulatory actions, and be repeated during ongoing monitoring. Analysts should assess the credibility, relevance and age of each finding, document how it affected the customer's risk rating, and escalate serious or unresolved matters to a designated senior reviewer.

### FFIEC-CDD-12 Handling Incomplete Due Diligence and Relationship Exit

Banks should define what happens when due diligence cannot be completed or when a customer declines to provide required information. Procedures should restrict new activity on the account, set a reasonable deadline for remediation, and require escalation to compliance staff. Where gaps remain unresolved, management should decide whether to exit the relationship and consider whether a suspicious activity referral is warranted. Each decision and its rationale should be recorded and available for examination.

### FFIEC-CDD-13 Documentation of Due Diligence Decisions

Banks should document the information gathered during customer due diligence, the risk rating assigned, and the reasoning behind any decision to accept, escalate or decline a relationship. Records should be clear enough that an independent reviewer can understand why a conclusion was reached without asking the original analyst. Banks should keep these records accessible for the full relationship and for a reasonable period afterward, and should record who approved exceptions and why.

### FFIEC-CDD-14 Consolidated Customer View Across Business Lines

Banks should maintain a consolidated view of each customer across products, business lines and affiliated entities, so that due diligence findings and risk ratings are not held in isolated systems. Where a customer holds several accounts or services, the bank should be able to see combined activity and apply the highest relevant risk consideration. Banks should define how due diligence information is shared internally and how conflicting ratings for the same customer are reconciled.

### FFIEC-CDD-15 Due Diligence on Trusts and Holding Vehicles

Banks should apply additional care when the customer is a trust, personal holding company or other vehicle that can obscure who controls the assets. Expected practice is to understand the purpose of the structure, the source of the funds placed in it, and the roles of settlors, trustees and those who benefit or exercise effective control. Where the structure has no evident business or personal rationale, the bank should escalate the relationship for enhanced review before opening accounts.

### FFIEC-CDD-16 Training and Quality Assurance for Due Diligence Staff

Banks should ensure that staff who collect and assess customer information are trained for their specific roles and understand how their work supports the risk rating. Training should be refreshed when products, customer segments or procedures change. Banks should also run a quality assurance program that samples completed files, measures error and rework rates, and reports recurring weaknesses to senior management so that corrective action is tracked through to closure.

## Third-Party Payment Processors

### FFIEC-TPPP-01 Processor Onboarding Due Diligence

Banks should perform documented due diligence before opening an account for a third-party payment processor. Reviews should confirm the processor's legal structure, beneficial owners, management background, licensing status, business model and the types of payments it handles. Banks should also check for adverse media, regulatory actions and prior account closures at other institutions. The depth of review should match the risk the relationship presents, and the results should be retained and available to examiners.

### FFIEC-TPPP-02 Contract Terms Allocating Compliance Duties

Banks should document the processor relationship in a written agreement that sets out each party's compliance responsibilities. The agreement should require the processor to follow applicable financial-crime requirements, to disclose changes in its client base or activities, and to give the bank timely access to transaction records and underlying client information on request. It should also state the circumstances in which the bank may suspend processing, hold funds or end the relationship.

### FFIEC-TPPP-03 Monitoring Returns and Unauthorized Transaction Rates

Banks should track return rates, chargebacks and customer complaints associated with each processor relationship, and compare them with reasonable thresholds and peer norms. Unusually high or rising rates of returned or disputed items, particularly those marked unauthorized, may signal fraud or deceptive practices by the processor's clients. Banks should investigate the causes promptly, document the findings, and escalate results that remain unexplained to appropriate senior staff for a decision on next steps.

### FFIEC-TPPP-05 Verifying the Processor's Own Client Controls

Banks should understand how a processor screens and monitors the merchants or originators it serves, since the bank usually has no direct relationship with them. This includes reviewing the processor's client acceptance procedures, its ongoing monitoring methods and its handling of high-risk business types. Banks should obtain evidence that these controls operate as described, such as sample client files, and should use audit or independent testing rights to confirm them periodically.

### FFIEC-TPPP-06 Red Flags and Suspicious Activity Escalation

Banks should train staff to recognize warning signs in processor relationships and to escalate them for suspicious activity review. Examples include a processor that resists providing client information, routes payments through many unrelated accounts, uses frequent changes of client names, or shows activity inconsistent with its stated business. Escalation procedures should set clear timelines, assign responsibility for investigation, and ensure that decisions and supporting reasoning are recorded.

### FFIEC-TPPP-07 Periodic Relationship Review and Exit Decisions

Banks should review each processor relationship on a periodic, risk-based schedule and whenever its activities change materially. Reviews should re-examine the original due diligence, monitoring results, complaints and any control weaknesses found. Where risks cannot be adequately managed, banks should have a defined process for restricting services, requiring remediation or exiting the relationship in an orderly way, while continuing to meet reporting obligations throughout the wind-down.

### FFIEC-TPPP-08 Nested Processors and Layered Relationships

Banks should identify whether a payment processor routes items through other processors, resellers or sub-processors, and should understand each layer's role in the payment chain. Where nesting exists, the bank should obtain information on the downstream parties, confirm that the primary processor applies comparable oversight to them, and treat limited visibility into end originators as a factor that raises inherent risk and calls for tighter controls or restricted volumes.

### FFIEC-TPPP-09 Settlement Accounts and Funds Flow Transparency

Banks should map how funds move between the processor, its clients and the bank, including the structure of settlement, reserve and pooled accounts. Management should be able to trace activity to the underlying originators, understand when funds are released, and assess whether account structures could obscure beneficial ownership or the source of funds. Arrangements that limit the bank's ability to see individual originator activity should be escalated and justified before approval.

### FFIEC-TPPP-10 Management Information and Board Reporting

Banks should provide senior management and the board with regular, meaningful reporting on processor relationships. Reports should cover the number and size of relationships, concentration of volume, exceptions to policy, open findings, and material changes in risk profile. Reporting should be clear enough to support informed decisions about risk appetite, resource needs and whether specific relationships remain consistent with the bank's stated tolerance for financial crime risk.

### FFIEC-TPPP-11 Training for Relationship and Compliance Staff

Banks should ensure that staff who manage, approve or monitor processor relationships receive training suited to their roles. Training should address how processors operate, typical misuse patterns, the warning signs that distinguish higher-risk activity, and when and how to escalate concerns. Relationship managers should understand that commercial objectives do not override compliance obligations, and training content should be refreshed as products and typologies change.

### FFIEC-TPPP-12 Independent Testing of the Processor Program

Banks should subject their processor oversight program to periodic independent testing by qualified personnel who are not involved in day-to-day operations. Testing should evaluate whether onboarding, monitoring and escalation practices follow written procedures, whether risk ratings are supported, and whether identified weaknesses are tracked to closure. Results should be reported to senior management, and the scope should be adjusted when the bank's processor footprint grows.

### FFIEC-TPPP-13 Cross-Border Processor Exposure and Data Access

Banks should consider additional risk when a processor serves originators or beneficiaries in other countries, or stores relevant records abroad. Banks should confirm that transaction and client information can be obtained promptly on request, that local legal restrictions on data sharing are understood, and that monitoring reflects the higher-risk corridors involved. Exposure to jurisdictions with weak controls should prompt enhanced scrutiny or limits on activity.

### FFIEC-TPPP-14 Reserve Arrangements and Exposure Limits

Banks should establish reserve or collateral arrangements proportionate to the credit, fraud and return risk that a payment processor presents, and should set clear exposure limits on the volume and value of items the processor may submit. Reserve levels and limits should be reviewed whenever the processor's client mix, product scope or return experience changes materially. Banks should document the rationale for each setting and escalate requests for higher limits to senior risk management rather than approving them at the relationship level.

## Electronic Banking

### FFIEC-EB-01 Remote Customer Onboarding and Identity Assurance

Banks that open accounts entirely through digital channels should apply identity verification measures proportionate to the absence of face-to-face contact. Controls should combine document authentication, data-source corroboration, and device and network signals, and should define when an application is escalated for manual review. Management should test how well these controls detect synthetic identities and altered documents, and should reassess them whenever the onboarding flow, data provider, or applicant population changes materially.

### FFIEC-EB-02 Authentication Strength and Account Takeover Monitoring

Banks should align the strength of customer authentication with the risk of the electronic services offered. Higher-risk activity, such as changing contact details, adding payees, or raising transfer limits, should require stronger or additional verification. Monitoring should look for signs of account takeover, including credential resets followed by rapid fund movement, unfamiliar devices, and abrupt changes in session behavior, and alerts should feed the suspicious activity review process.

### FFIEC-EB-03 Oversight of Third-Party Digital Service Providers

When a bank relies on a technology vendor, fintech partner, or processor to deliver electronic banking services, it remains responsible for the financial crime risk those services create. Banks should perform due diligence on the provider's compliance capabilities before onboarding, define in the agreement who monitors activity and who reports concerns, obtain access to relevant data, and review the provider's performance periodically against agreed expectations.

### FFIEC-EB-04 Monitoring Calibration for Real-Time Payments

Banks offering instant or near-instant electronic payments should recognize that reduced settlement time leaves little opportunity to recall funds after the fact. Monitoring should therefore include pre-transaction or in-flight checks suited to the speed of the service, with thresholds and scenarios calibrated to observed typologies such as rapid pass-through activity and mule account behavior. Management should validate these settings before launch and following significant volume growth.

### FFIEC-EB-05 Cross-Border Access to Electronic Banking Services

Because online and mobile services can be reached from almost anywhere, banks should assess the risk that customers or third parties access accounts from locations outside the markets the bank intends to serve. Controls should use location indicators, such as network address and device data, to identify access from higher-risk or unsupported locations, and should define restrictions, enhanced review, or reporting steps when such access is detected or when the bank expands into a new market.

### FFIEC-EB-06 Risk Assessment for Digital-Only Business Customers

Before extending electronic banking to a new category of business customers that will never visit a branch, banks should update their risk assessment to reflect how those customers transact. The assessment should consider expected payment volumes, ownership transparency, the nature of the business, and the ability to verify legitimacy remotely. Resulting expectations for ongoing due diligence and account activity profiles should be documented and approved by appropriate management.

### FFIEC-EB-07 Remote Deposit Capture Risk Controls

Banks offering remote deposit capture should assess the risk of the service before launch and whenever limits or eligible customers change. Expected controls include customer eligibility screening, deposit limits aligned to expected activity, detection of duplicate or altered item images, and review of unusual deposit patterns across accounts. Banks should also monitor for rapid movement of deposited funds to third parties and should document how exceptions are escalated for investigation.

### FFIEC-EB-08 Device and Session Intelligence for Linked Accounts

Banks should use device and session signals to help identify relationships between electronic banking accounts that would otherwise appear unconnected. Useful signals include shared device identifiers, repeated access from the same network, use of anonymizing tools, and automated session behavior. Management should define how these signals feed monitoring and investigations, test them for false positives, and ensure that linked-account findings are considered when reviewing customer risk ratings.

### FFIEC-EB-09 Governance of API and Data-Sharing Access

Banks that expose application programming interfaces or permit customer-authorized data sharing should govern that access as part of their financial crime risk program. Management should maintain an inventory of connected parties, define the data and functions each may use, require strong consent and credential handling, and monitor connection volumes and behavior for abuse. Banks should be able to suspend a connection promptly when misuse or control weaknesses are identified.

### FFIEC-EB-10 Detecting Mule Activity in Digital Accounts

Banks should design monitoring to detect money mule activity in accounts opened and operated entirely online. Indicators include newly opened accounts receiving numerous unrelated inbound payments, funds moved out shortly after arrival, activity inconsistent with stated purpose, and account details shared across applicants. Banks should combine onboarding data with transaction behavior, set clear escalation paths, and use confirmed cases to refine scenarios and customer education.

## Funds Transfers

### FFIEC-FT-01 Assessing Inherent Risk in Funds Transfer Activity

Banks should assess the inherent risk of their funds transfer services by considering transaction volume, average and peak values, the speed of settlement, and the mix of domestic and cross-border flows. Services that permit high-value, same-day or irrevocable transfers generally carry greater exposure and warrant stronger preventive and detective controls. The assessment should be documented, refreshed when the service changes materially, and used to set monitoring intensity and staffing.

### FFIEC-FT-02 Payment Message Information Integrity

Banks should ensure that originator and beneficiary information is captured accurately at initiation and carried unchanged through each stage of the payment chain. Systems should reject or hold messages with missing, truncated or obviously meaningless party details, and staff should not strip or alter fields to speed processing. Management should periodically test message samples to confirm that required information remains complete and that exceptions are investigated and resolved promptly.

### FFIEC-FT-03 Cross-Border Corridor and Country Risk

Banks should evaluate the risk of each cross-border funds transfer corridor they support, considering the sanctions posture, corruption and secrecy levels, and the strength of anti-money laundering oversight in the counterparty country. Corridors with elevated risk should receive enhanced review of payment purpose, tighter monitoring thresholds, and senior approval before launch. The corridor assessment should be revisited whenever conditions in a destination or origin country change significantly.

### FFIEC-FT-04 Intermediary and Correspondent Payment Transparency

Banks that route transfers through correspondent or intermediary institutions should understand how those parties handle payment instructions and should expect full transparency of underlying party information, including in cover payment arrangements. Due diligence should address the intermediary's own controls and its downstream relationships. Where an intermediary cannot or will not pass through complete information, the bank should escalate, restrict the routing, or end the arrangement.

### FFIEC-FT-05 Monitoring for Pass-Through and Rapid Movement

Banks should monitor funds transfer activity for patterns inconsistent with a customer's expected profile, such as funds received and sent onward within a short period, repeated transfers just below internal review levels, or many unrelated counterparties sending to one account. Monitoring rules should be tuned using risk assessment results, alerts should be investigated by trained staff, and decisions about suspicious activity reporting should be recorded with clear rationale.

### FFIEC-FT-06 Controls over Digital and Third-Party Initiation

Banks should apply risk-based controls to channels through which customers or partners initiate transfers, including online banking, mobile applications, and programmatic connections offered through third-party providers. Expected controls include strong authentication, limits suited to the customer type, device and session anomaly checks, and contractual rights to obtain information from the provider. Management should reassess channel risk whenever a new initiation method or provider is introduced.

### FFIEC-FT-07 Funds Transfer Recordkeeping and Retrieval

Banks should keep complete records of funds transfers and be able to retrieve them promptly when requested by internal reviewers, auditors or authorities. Records should capture the originator, beneficiary, amounts, dates, instructions received and the institutions involved. Retention periods and storage formats should be defined by policy, protected from alteration, and tested regularly so that information remains accessible after system migrations, process changes or the onboarding of new payment processing providers.

### FFIEC-FT-08 Due Diligence on High-Volume Wire Customers

Banks should apply enhanced due diligence to customers whose business models involve frequent or high-value wire activity, such as trading firms, payment facilitators and import-export businesses. Diligence should establish the expected purpose, counterparties, volumes and geographies of payments, and should be refreshed when activity departs from that profile. Account opening for such customers should require approval from a senior reviewer who understands the related funds transfer risks.

### FFIEC-FT-09 Alert Investigation and Escalation Standards

Banks should maintain documented standards for investigating alerts generated on funds transfer activity. Investigators should review the customer profile, related accounts, counterparties and payment history, and record the reasoning behind each disposition. Alerts that remain unexplained should be escalated to a designated decision-maker within set timeframes. Management should track backlogs, ageing and quality assurance results, and should adjust staffing when investigative capacity does not keep pace with alert volumes.

### FFIEC-FT-10 Training for Payment Operations Personnel

Banks should provide role-specific training to staff who originate, review, release or repair funds transfers. Training should cover common typologies, red flags in payment instructions, the handling of manual overrides and the steps for escalating concerns. Content should be updated when products, systems or payment channels change, and completion should be tracked. Staff in high-risk roles should receive more frequent and practical instruction, including case-based exercises.

### FFIEC-FT-11 Handling of Returned, Recalled and Rejected Payments

Banks should control how returned, recalled, amended and rejected funds transfers are processed, since repeated reversals and redirections can indicate attempts to disguise the source or destination of funds. Procedures should require that funds be returned only to the original source, that changes to beneficiary details be independently verified, and that patterns of frequent reversals be reported to monitoring and investigation teams for review.

### FFIEC-FT-12 Independent Testing of Funds Transfer Controls

Banks should subject funds transfer controls to periodic independent testing by qualified personnel who are not involved in daily operations. Testing should evaluate whether monitoring scenarios, screening tools, approval limits and recordkeeping work as designed and cover the bank's actual risk exposure. Findings should be reported to senior management and the board, with remediation owners and deadlines, and follow-up confirming that corrective actions were effective.

## Nonbank Financial Institutions / MSBs

### FFIEC-MSB-01 Risk Assessment of Money Services Business Customers

Banks should assess the inherent risk of each money services business customer before and after account opening, rather than treating the whole category as uniformly high or low risk. Relevant factors include the range of services offered, transaction volumes and values, the customer base served, the maturity of the business, and the strength of its own compliance program. The resulting rating should drive the depth of due diligence and the intensity of monitoring applied.

### FFIEC-MSB-02 Onboarding Due Diligence for Money Services Businesses

Banks should collect enough information at onboarding to understand who owns and controls a money services business and how it earns revenue. Expected practice includes confirming registration and any required state licensing, reviewing ownership and management, obtaining a description of services and customer types, and sighting the written compliance program. Where the prospective customer cannot or will not provide this information, the bank should decline the relationship or escalate the decision to senior compliance staff.

### FFIEC-MSB-03 Service and Product Mix Risk Factors

Banks should evaluate how the specific services a money services business offers affect its exposure to illicit finance. Domestic money transmission, currency exchange, check cashing, and stored-value or prepaid issuance each present different speed, anonymity, and transaction-size characteristics. Banks should document which services the customer provides, note where product features such as higher load limits or anonymous use increase risk, and tailor expected controls to those features.

### FFIEC-MSB-04 Cross-Border Corridor and Geographic Exposure

Banks should understand the countries and corridors through which a money services business sends or receives funds, and should weigh them in the customer's risk rating. Greater attention is warranted where flows involve jurisdictions with weak supervision, elevated corruption, sanctions exposure, or known conflict-related financing risks. Banks should ask for corridor-level volume information, compare it with the stated business profile, and reassess the rating when the corridor mix changes materially.

### FFIEC-MSB-05 Ongoing Monitoring of Money Services Business Accounts

Banks should monitor money services business accounts against an expected activity profile set at onboarding and refreshed periodically. Monitoring should look for unexplained changes in volume, structuring patterns, rapid movement of funds in and out, unusual counterparties, and activity inconsistent with the stated customer base. Alerts should be reviewed by trained staff, decisions documented, and suspicious activity escalated promptly through the bank's established reporting process.

### FFIEC-MSB-06 Evaluating the Customer's Compliance Program and Testing

Banks should form a view on the quality of a money services business customer's own anti-money-laundering program, since the bank relies on it to manage risk upstream. Reasonable steps include reviewing its policies, the designated compliance officer's role, staff training, and the results of independent testing. Material deficiencies or unresolved findings should prompt enhanced monitoring, remediation requests with deadlines, and, where necessary, a documented decision to restrict or close the account.

### FFIEC-MSB-07 Pooled Account and Funds Commingling Transparency

Banks should understand how a money services business uses pooled or omnibus accounts to hold funds for its own customers. Banks should determine whether the business commingles customer funds with operating funds, whether it can identify the underlying originators and beneficiaries on request, and how it reconciles balances. Where transparency is limited, banks should apply tighter transaction limits, request supporting records more often, and escalate the relationship for senior review.

### FFIEC-MSB-08 Virtual Currency and Digital Asset Exposure

Banks should assess whether a money services business customer exchanges, transmits, or custodies virtual currency, since such activity can raise anonymity and speed-of-movement concerns. Banks should expect the customer to show how it verifies users, screens wallet addresses, and monitors on-chain activity for links to illicit sources. Banks should document the expected volumes and counterparties and reassess the relationship whenever the customer adds a new digital asset service.

### FFIEC-MSB-09 Sanctions Screening Across Payment Flows

Banks should confirm that money services business customers screen their own clients, counterparties, and payment messages against applicable sanctions lists before funds are released. Banks should also screen the customer's transactions that pass through the bank, and should review how the customer resolves potential matches, records its decisions, and reports blocked or rejected items. Weak screening logic or delayed list updates should prompt corrective action and closer oversight of the account.

### FFIEC-MSB-10 Completeness of Originator and Beneficiary Information

Banks should expect money services businesses to capture and pass along complete originator and beneficiary information with the payments they send through the bank. Banks should test samples of payment messages for missing, truncated, or implausible fields, and should treat repeated gaps as a red flag. Where the customer relies on intermediaries, banks should confirm that the data remains intact through each handoff and can be produced promptly for investigators.

### FFIEC-MSB-11 Suspicious Activity Escalation for MSB Relationships

Banks should maintain a clear path for escalating unusual activity in money services business accounts to investigators and senior management. Alerts should be reviewed against the customer's stated business profile, with attention to structuring patterns, rapid movement of funds, and third-party deposits. Banks should document the rationale for filing or not filing a report, and should decide at defined intervals whether continuing the relationship remains within risk appetite.

### FFIEC-MSB-12 Account Exit Decisions and Risk-Based Retention

Banks should make decisions to retain or exit money services business relationships through a documented, risk-based process rather than by blanket categorization. Each decision should weigh the customer's demonstrated controls, the bank's ability to manage the residual risk, and the quality of the customer's cooperation. Banks should record the reasons for an exit, coordinate timing with relevant control functions, and report exit trends to governance committees.

## Foreign Correspondent Accounts (summary)

### FFIEC-FCA-01 Risk Assessment of Foreign Correspondent Relationships

Banks should assess the inherent risk of each foreign correspondent relationship before opening the account and refresh that assessment as the relationship changes. The assessment should weigh the respondent's customer base, the products and services it will use, the countries where it operates, and the channels through which it submits activity. Banks should document the rating rationale and align the depth of due diligence and monitoring with the resulting risk level.

### FFIEC-FCA-02 Due Diligence on Respondent Institutions

Banks should perform due diligence that establishes who owns and manages each respondent institution, how it is licensed and supervised in its home country, and what the respondent's own financial-crime program looks like. Reviewers should consider the respondent's reputation, past enforcement history, and the quality of its policies. Banks should keep the resulting file current and ensure that senior approval precedes activation of the relationship.

### FFIEC-FCA-03 Nested Accounts and Downstream Clearing Exposure

Banks should identify whether a respondent offers its own services to other foreign financial institutions through the correspondent account, a practice often called nesting or downstream clearing. Where it does, banks should understand who those downstream parties are, what activity they conduct, and how the respondent screens them. Banks should restrict or decline relationships in which the respondent cannot provide meaningful transparency about these underlying users.

### FFIEC-FCA-04 Ongoing Monitoring of Correspondent Transaction Flows

Banks should monitor correspondent activity on an ongoing basis, comparing actual wire and payment flows with the expected profile recorded at onboarding. Monitoring should look for unusual volumes, rapid movement of funds, repeated round-value payments, and activity involving unrelated third parties or unexpected countries. Scenarios and thresholds should be calibrated to the respondent's risk rating, and alerts should be investigated and resolved within set timeframes.

### FFIEC-FCA-05 Enhanced Scrutiny for Higher-Risk Jurisdictions and Shell Banks

Banks should apply enhanced scrutiny to respondents located in or routing activity through jurisdictions with weak financial-crime controls, high corruption, or limited supervisory transparency. Additional measures may include site visits, independent reviews of the respondent's controls, and tighter transaction limits. Banks should not maintain relationships with shell banks and should confirm that respondents do not offer accounts to such entities.

### FFIEC-FCA-06 Escalation, Information Requests, and Relationship Exit

Banks should maintain a clear process for escalating concerns about a foreign correspondent, including timely requests for information to the respondent about specific transactions or customers. Responses that are late, incomplete, or evasive should be reviewed by senior compliance staff. Where risks cannot be mitigated, banks should restrict services or exit the relationship in an orderly manner and retain records supporting the decision.

### FFIEC-FCA-07 Payable-Through Account Controls and Sub-Account Holder Visibility

Banks should apply specific safeguards when offering payable-through arrangements that allow a respondent's own customers to transact directly through the bank's account. Management should understand who may draw on the account, confirm that the respondent performs comparable identification of those sub-account holders, and obtain identifying information on request. Banks should set limits on activity volume and permitted uses, test the respondent's controls periodically, and restrict or close the arrangement when transparency into underlying users is inadequate.

### FFIEC-FCA-08 Governance, Training, and Independent Testing of Correspondent Programs

Banks should ensure that the program for foreign correspondent activity is supported by clear governance, including defined ownership, board or senior management reporting, and adequate staffing. Personnel who open, review, or approve respondent relationships should receive role-specific training on typologies and red flags. Banks should subject the program to periodic independent testing, track remediation of findings to completion, and update procedures when products, vendors, or respondent profiles change materially.
